Autonomous vehicles have become a hot spot of the automotive industry, many cities have claimed that 
autonomous vehicles should be capable of recognizing gestures used by traﬃc police. Traditional traﬃc 
police gesture recognition methods rely on depth-sensor or wearable-devices, which limits their availabil- 
ity in the domain of the intelligent vehicle. Vision-based methods have fewer requirements for distance, 
but the modeling process is challenging due to the complexity of the visual scenes. Inspired by the recent 
success in vision-based pose estimation networks such as Convolutional Pose Machine (CPM), in this pa- 
per, we propose a novel vision-based human-machine interface to recognize eight kinds of Chinese traﬃc 
police gestures and apply it in the real-time recognition tasks. This method integrates a modiﬁed CPM 
network and two kinds of handcrafted features: Relative Bone Length and Angle with Gravity as spa- 
tial domain features, and adopt a Long short-term memory (LSTM) network to extract temporal domain 
features. To train and validate our method, we create a gestures dataset with two hours of traﬃc police 
gesture videos, which has 3354 gesture instances. The experiment results show that the proposed method 
is capable of recognizing traﬃc police gestures, and is fast enough for online gesture prediction.

1. Introduction 
With the fast growth of urbanization in China, traveling by car 
has become more and more popular. Recognizing gestures used by 
authorized people, such as police oﬃcers, is one of the essential 
skills of drivers. For example, when there are traﬃc jams or traﬃc 
lights fail, traﬃc police usually controls traﬃc and guides drivers 
through their gestures. Facing the upcoming era of automatic driv- 
ing, many cities have begun to develop regulations to regulate au- 
tonomous vehicles, and one of the important concerns is that auto- 
driving vehicles should be capable of recognizing these gestures 
and dealing with them within a limited time. Therefore, the de- 
velopment of traﬃc police gesture recognition methods is one of 
the essentials of building driver-assistance systems for intelligent 
vehicles. 
The Chinese Traﬃc Police Gestures system (namely CTPGs) is 
deﬁned and regulated by the Chinese Ministry of Public Security, 
of which the gestures are joint actions and movements of arms 
and torso. To recognize these gestures usually faces two challenges. 
Firstly, it is very diﬃcult to extract skeletal features from traﬃc

The second challenge is how to reduce or eliminate the effects

To address these issues and inspired by the work of [1] , in 
this paper, we design a novel recognition method, which applies 
a modiﬁed CPM to extract skeletal data, then converts them with 
two kinds of handcrafted spatial features: Relative Bone Length 
and Angle with Gravity, and ﬁnally sends the spatial features into 
a Long short-term memory (LSTM) network [2] to recognize the 
class of traﬃc police gestures. The contributions of our work can 
be summarized as follows. 
• First, we produce the Police Gesture Dataset, which contains 
the videos of Chinese traﬃc police commanding gestures, and 
ground truth gesture labels for each video frame. 
• Second, we propose a method to extract two kinds of hand- 
crafted features from human skeletal features, which acts as a

2. Related works 
Because of the importance of traﬃc police gesture recogni- 
tion, a lot of studies have been carried out. These studies can 
mainly be divided into two categories: the wearable sensor based 
method, and the vision sensor based method. The wearable sen- 
sor based method uses inertial MEMS (micro electro-mechanical 
systems) sensors (e. g. accelerometers and gyroscopes) to measure 
the movements of police arms, and inferences the poses according 
to those movements. For instance, Wang and Yuan [3] applied two 
accelerometers on hands to classify the command gesture of the 
traﬃc police. Yuan and Wang [4] used digital gloves which wear- 
able sensors were ﬁxed on the back of the police hands to extract 
the gesture data. Although these methods can get a good recogni- 
tion rate, and are capable of working in a long range, they require 
the police to wear the designated devices, which is an extra hin- 
drance in practical situations. Additionally, the relatively high cost 
of the devices also limits its use in traﬃc police gesture recogni- 
tion. 
Vision sensor based methods mainly include two steps: The 
ﬁrst step acquires the gesture video from a digital camera, then ex- 
tracts spatial features from the video. The second step is to classify 
gesture based upon the spatial features. Vision sensor based meth- 
ods have been widely used in gesture recognition, and achieved 
both scientiﬁc and economic success because of its convenience 
and relatively low cost. For example, Guo et al. [5,6] used a max- 
covering scheme to recognize gestures of Chinese traﬃc police. The 
scheme mainly based on the observation that body-part tiles max- 
imally cover the foreground region and suit a body plan. Then 
the rotation joint angle or Gabor feature based two-dimensional 
(2D) principal component analysis (PCA) is used to recognize the 
command gestures. For this method, RGB camera is used to cap- 
ture testing images from video, and a 5-part body model is con- 
structed according to a 2D model. Therefore, it is diﬃcult to deal 
with the situation when traﬃc police arms are perpendicular to 
the image plane. Eichner et al. [7,8] proposed a method to evalu- 
ate the spatial layout of humans (namely the position of the head, 
torso and arms) in static images. Once a person is identiﬁed by 
the upper-body detector, the method for searching their body parts 
can be simpliﬁed via the constraints on position and appearance 
according to that detection. Besides, Kang et al. [9] combined ges- 
ture spotting with gesture recognition, and classiﬁed 10 kinds of 
upper-body gestures as the interface between a video game (Quake 
II) and its users. Song et al. [10] presented an uniﬁed framework 
for body and hand tracking so as to understand simultaneously 
performed body-and-hand gestures. In addition, they introduced 
a multi-signal gesture database (namely, the NATOPS aircraft han- 
dling signals) including 24 body-and-hand gestures. 
Being integrated with a depth sensor, Microsoft Kinect [11] can 
build 3D models for objects within 5 m, as well as extracting depth 
information, locating human skeletons and ﬁltering backgrounds. 
It has been widely used on pose recognition. For example, Guo 
et al. [12] proposed a method that recognizes 8 kinds of traﬃc

police gestures via Kinect based on static and dynamic descrip- 
tor fusion, and used a mean structural similarity index to recog- 
nize traﬃc police gestures. Le et al. [13] proposed a real-time traf- 
ﬁc gesture recognition test platform system based on support vec- 
tor machine (SVM) training data, in which traﬃc police command 
gestures are captured in the form of a depth image from a visual 
sensor, and the human skeleton is then constructed by a kinematic 
model. Zhou et al. [14] presented a real-time hand posture recog- 
nition method in color-depth images, and the histogram of gradi- 
ent features of a hand posture was computed for Extreme Learn- 
ing Machine classiﬁer to recognize different postures. Though the 
integrated vision sensor helps to provide a more precise position 
of human parts, and improves the accuracy of gesture recognition. 
The working range of the depth sensor is limited, which results in 
the inﬂuence of the accuracy of gesture recognition when the dis- 
tance between Kinect and traﬃc police is large. 
The effectiveness of machine learning and deep learning has 
been demonstrated in many ﬁelds besides image classiﬁcation, 
such as image retrieval [15] and location recommendation [16] . 
Similarly, studies on deep-learning based solutions for pose esti- 
mation and action recognition ﬁelds have multiplied for the past 
few years [17] . The pose estimation methods produce 2D or 3D lo- 
cations of human parts. For example, Wei et al. [1] incorporated a 
convolutional network architecture into the pose machine frame- 
work so as to allow the learning of representations for both im- 
age and spatial relationships. This method provides a systematic 
approach to compose convolutional networks with large receptive 
ﬁelds for 2D pose estimation tasks. Cao et al. [18] used a convo- 
lutional neural network (CNNs) to learn the association (namely 
Part Aﬃnity Fields, PAFs) of body parts, and designed an archi- 
tecture to jointly learn 2D part locations and their association via 
two branches of the same sequential prediction process. The archi- 
tecture encodes global context, allowing a greedy bottom-up pars- 
ing step that maintains high accuracy while achieving a real-time 
performance, irrespective of the number of people in the image. 
Mehta et al. [19] presented a single RGB camera-based method 
that yields temporally stable 3D global pose reconstructions using 
a convolutional network. They used the temporal history of estima- 
tion sequence as features to ensure smooth tracking of coordinates 
over time. 
Action recognition tasks aim to recognize single or multiple 
actions occurred in a sequence of images. In recent years, deep 
learning solutions for action recognition tasks are widely stud- 
ied. For example, Bilen et al. [20] summarize the dynamics of a 
entire video sequence as an image, and train a dynamic-aware 
network for action recognition. This method is powerful and ef- 
ﬁcient against ﬁxed-length video sets with one action per video, 
such as UCF101 [21] and HMDB51 [22] , but it can not be ap- 
plied for datasets with multiple actions per video. Donahue et al. 
[23] present Long-term Recurrent Convolutional Networks (LRCNs), 
which extracts visual features from input by CNNs, then sends 
those features into an LSTM sequence to predict the actions. The 
advantage of LRCNs is that the prediction length is variable, which 
is suitable for handling arbitrarily long sequences. Zhang et al. 
[24] transfer the features in optical ﬂow CNN to that of motion 
vectors CNN in each frame, and managed to achieve real-time per- 
formance in action recognition tasks. However, LRCNs and motion 
vectors CNN learn massive feature representations from images di- 
rectly, which weakens the attention of the network to human. 
Our method relies on extracting the skeletal data from monoc- 
ular RGB images to separate the human from the background, so it 
is a skeleton based method. There are already many skeleton based 
methods for gesture recognition, this paragraph discusses the rea- 
son of not using them to recognize traﬃc police gestures. Zhu et al. 
[25] published a survey to describe the evolution of action recog- 
nition methods, which covers some skeleton-based approaches, but

the marker-based system requires the target to wear special suits, 
which is not realistic in practice. As for the multiple views recon- 
struction systems, according to relevant article [30] , they still lack 
enough range to detect police on road. Therefore, although there 
are many methods aim to recognize gestures, few of them can be 
referenced considering the restrictions caused by the police gesture 
recognition task. 
3. Methodology 
This section introduces eight kinds of Chinese Traﬃc Police Ges- 
tures, then describes the extraction of the handcrafted spatial fea- 
tures in details, and explains the extraction of temporal features 
using the sequence of spatial features as input. Finally, for each 
frame, a gesture class is inferred from the temporal features. 
3.1. Chinese traﬃc police gestures 
The Chinese Traﬃc Police Gestures are composed by 8 kinds of 
gestures: (1) stop, (2) move straight, (3) left turn, (4) left turn wait- 
ing, (5) right turn, (6) lane changing, (7) slow down, (8) pull over. 
An extra gesture (0) “stand in attention” is used in this paper to 
describe the situation of which a police does not change the cur- 
rent traﬃc. 
Fig. 1 illustrates actions of Chinese Traﬃc Police Gestures. These 
gestures are explained as follow: (1) To express “stop”, the po-

lice faces the vehicle and raises his left arm. (2) To express “move 
straight”, the police stands laterally to the traﬃc, extends his both 
arms, then bends his right arm. (3) To express “left turn”, the po- 
lice raises his right arm, then swings his left arm from side to cen- 
ter of body. (4) To express “left turn waiting”, the police stands lat- 
erally to the traﬃc, then swings his left arm up and down. (5) To 
express “right turn”, the police raises his left arm, then swings his 
right arm from side to center of body. (6) To express “lane chang- 
ing”, the police extends his right arm, parallel to the right shoul- 
der, then swings to the left. (7) To express “slow down”, the police 
extends his right arm, parallel to the right shoulder, then swings 
down. (8) To express “pull over”, the police extends his right arm, 
parallel to the waist, then raises left arm, and swing right arm to 
the left. 
3.2. Extraction of spatial features 
The overall process of spatial feature extraction from input im- 
age to spatial features is shown in Fig. 2 . Fig. 2 includes 5 subﬁg- 
ures connected with arrows above them. Arrows denote the pro- 
cesses of handling spatial features, and subﬁgures denote the out- 
puts of these processes. Subﬁgure (a) shows an input image frame 
with a police making gestures, the small yellow dots denote the 
parts of this police. The part in this article refers to the anatomi- 
cal landmarks of a person, it’s also called joint or keypoint accord- 
ing to different papers. Subﬁgure (b) illustrates the absolute coor- 
dinate position of the parts by big yellow dots. These dots are col- 
lectively known as the articulated pose of the person, which is the 
collection of the absolute part positions on the image. There are 
totally P part positions, marked as Y p . The arrow between (a) and 
(b), marked as process g T , is used to predict articulated pose of the 
police from the RGB image. In this article, g T is implemented as a 
convolutional network modiﬁed from Convolutional Pose Machine 
(CPM) [1] . Subﬁgure (c) illustrates the Bone Vectors. In this ﬁgure, 
the parts (yellow dots) are connected by green arrows with mark 
S c . This paper deﬁnes the pattern of how these parts are connected 
with each others as Connection Dependency. According to the Con- 
nection Dependency, there are totally C connections between parts, 
each of the connection c ∈ {1, ..., C } contains 2 parts, namely start- 
ing part m and ending part n, m, n ∈ P . Based on the Connection 
Dependency, the Bone Vectors S are deﬁned as vectors pointing

To implement the ﬁrst process g T in Fig. 2 , a modiﬁed Convo- 
lutional Pose Machine (CPM) network is applied. The input of CPM 
is the set of colors from each position ( u, v ) of an image, denoted 
by Z , Z ⊂ R 2 . The pixel position of each part in the image is de- 
noted by Y p , Y p ∈ Z . A person have P parts in total, the position of 
parts in the image ( Y 1 , ..., Y p ) is denoted by Y. g t ( ·) is a classi- 
ﬁer, which is used to predict the conﬁdence of a point z belonging 
to part Y p . The value of conﬁdence is denoted by b(Y p = z) , z ∈ Z . 
The subscript of the classiﬁer t ∈ 0, ..., T denotes the stage of pose 
estimation. Each stage g t ( ·) can be seen as an independent classi- 
ﬁer, with different convolutional architectures and identical losses. 
Classiﬁer of each stage gains a larger receptive ﬁeld than that of 
previous stage, and outputs a reﬁned prediction. At the 1 st stage, 
when t = 1 , classiﬁer produces following conﬁdence map: 
g 1 (x z ) → { b p (Y p = z) } p∈{ 1 ,..., 2 P} (1) 
In Eq. (1) , as the input, x z denotes the features at image loca- 
tion z . b p (Y p = z) denotes the conﬁdence score map of location z 
belonging to part p . The conﬁdence score map can be seen as an 
array with each element representing the evidence score of a pixel 
in the image belonging to a human part, or belonging to the back- 
ground. P denotes the total number of parts, the set {1, ..., 2 P } de- 
notes P conﬁdence score maps of a pixel belonging to a part, and 
P conﬁdence score maps of a pixel not belonging to a part. 
Following paragraph describes our modiﬁcation on the CPM. In 
the original CPM paper, the network only produces P + 1 conﬁ- 
dence score maps for P parts and 1 background (namely style (a)), 
so the conﬁdence score map set is { b p (Y p = z) } p∈{ 0 ,...,P} , in which 
the b 0 represents the background scores. In their later work [18] on 
CVPR, the PAFs network increases the number of maps to 2 ×P 
for P parts and P backgrounds (namely style (b)). The difference is 
that in style (a), each part score competes with the scores of other 
parts and the score of the background to decide the probability of 
whether a pixel belongs to this part, while in style (b), since each 
part has it’s own background score, it only competes with it’s own 
background to produce a probability, and will not be affected by 
the score of other parts. In style (a), the probabilities of a pixel be- 
longing to each part and the background collectively add up to one, 
while in style (b), the probabilities of each two scores: the score of 
a pixel belonging to part p and the score of this pixel not belong- 
ing to part p collectively add up to one. Style (b) takes advantage 
when multiple parts are close or overlapping, and the most obvi- 
ous part gains extremely high scores around it’s area. In this case, 
if style (a) is applied, then the score of this obvious part will sup- 
press other parts around it’s area, so only this one particular part 
will be found. However, if style (b) is applied, then the scores will 
not be affected by each others, so the occluded parts may still able 
to be found as long as their conﬁdences are larger than the global 
threshold. In this article, we modify the Convolutional Pose Ma- 
chine network to make it compatible with the network style (b).

3.3. Extraction of temporal features 
Gestures consist of temporally consecutive actions, ignoring the 
temporal consistency may cause false classiﬁcations. Fig. 4 gives 
an example of two actions with similar spatial features belong- 
ing to different gestures, which requires the recognition method 
to further classify the temporal features. This ﬁgure compares the 
spatial features of two gestures (namely Stop and Pull Over), each 
gesture contains two consecutive actions. The RGB images at the 
bottom show the input frame, the black background images at the 
top show the Bone Vectors of two gestures. It is clear that the ﬁrst 
actions of two gestures possess similar spatial features, and may 
confuse the classiﬁer if the spatial features were the only avail- 
able evidence. In this case, the best way to distinguish these two 
gestures is to infer the current gesture by looking at the spatial 
features from the past. In this article, the substantial evidences 
deduced from the spatial features of previous times are denoted 
by Temporal Features. Since each frame includes some new spa- 
tial features into the substantial evidences, their temporal features 
are always different with each others. These temporal features is 
ultimately used to decide the class of current gesture.
