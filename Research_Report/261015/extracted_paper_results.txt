4.1. Convolutional network for part localization 
To localize the human parts for spatial feature extraction, we 
introduce the Convolutional Pose Machine (CPM) and modiﬁed it’s 
architecture. In details, we increase the number of score maps to 
acquire better performence when parts are overlapping, and de- 
crease the stages to make the execution time shorter. To distin- 
guish our modiﬁed architecture from original CPM, we reference 
our architecture as Police Keypoint Extraction Network (PKEN) in 
the following sections. 
The architecture of PKEN is illustrated in Fig. 5 . In the ﬁgure, C 
represents a convolutional layer, P represents a max pooling layer, 
L represents the loss. The top 10 convolutional layers of the net- 
work are the same as that of the VGG-19. The top 12 convolu- 
tional layers implements ψ( ·), which extract image features from 
original image. There are 3 pooling layers among these convolu- 
tional layers, which means that the extracted image features are 
8 times smaller in width and height than the original image. The 
13 th to 17 th convolutional layers implement g 1 ( ·), the stage 1 of 
CPM. It takes x z as input, and outputs conﬁdence set b 1 . The 18 th 
to 24 th convolutional layers implement g 2 ( ·), the stage 2 of classi- 
ﬁer, which accepts two inputs: x z and b 1 as input, and produces b 2 . 
Similarly, The 25 th to 31 th convolutional layers implement g 3 ( ·).

5.2. Label preprocessing 
Before training, the appearance time of each label in Police Ges- 
ture Dataset is delayed for 750 ms after the occurrence of gestures. 
This time delay is essential for the entire pipeline to work properly, 
and dramatically increases its accuracy. 
Fig. 11 illustrates how labels are delayed. the ﬁgure illustrated a 
few frames clipped from the dataset, with annotated labels. There 
are three rows named image, original labels and delayed labels. 
The original labels and the delayed labels use gray area to express 
one kind of gesture: Stand In Attention, and use green area to ex- 
press another kind of gesture: Pull Over. Originally, the Pull Over 
label appears as soon as the police raises his hand. After delay- 
ing the labels, the Stand In Attention area becomes longer, which 
means that the appearances of Pull Over labels are delayed. The 
reason of delaying the training labels is: originally, a label ap- 
pears as soon as the gesture occurred. If the original labels are 
used to train the network, it means that the network has to re- 
sponse immediately as soon as a gesture occurred, which makes 
the network over-sensitive against each small movement of the po- 
lice, and makes wrong predictions frequently. Therefore, the tim- 
ing of labels are delayed to leave some time for the LSTM to ob- 
serve, so the network will not judge the meaning of the gesture 
until the police made enough movements. It turns out that delay- 
ing the appearance of labels signiﬁcantly improved the accuracy 
of the network, and also made it robust against small movements 
that is not related to any gestures. At the cost, delayed training 
labels decrease the method response speed. A comparison of per- 
formances between human and our method trained with delayed 
labels is given at the results section.

In step (1), to establish the PKEN network, we build the net-

training, we choose a batch size of 15, and adopt Adam optimizer 
[40] for gradient descent, with a learning rate of 0.0 0 08 and an 
exponential decaying rate of 0.8 for every 20,0 0 0 steps. Data aug- 
mentation methods such as rotation and scaling are applied to in- 
crease the variety of dataset. We train the PKEN for 50 epochs. 
In step (2), we run the forward pass of the trained PKEN to ex- 
tract part location from Police Gesture Dataset into spatial feature 
F . PKEN parses the police gesture videos frame-by-frame, gener- 
ates a sequence of spatial features. The temporal labels marked for 
the Police Gesture Dataset is unchanged and used to mark ground 
truth gestures for these spatial features. 
In step (3), to establish the LSTM network, we build the net- 
work in Fig. 7 , and initialize all the neurons with Xavier initializer 
[41] . No data augmentation method is applied. As for training, we 
randomly clip the sequence of spatial features into smaller frag- 
ments. Each of the fragment is a video with 90 seconds duration, 
and 128 fragments compose a batch. We adopt Adam optimizer 
for truncated back propagation, with a learning rate of 0.0 0 04, and 
train the network for 50,0 0 0 steps. 
After the training, feature extractor and classiﬁer are connected 
in order to form the Traﬃc Police Gesture Recognizer. The entire 
network takes a sequence of images as input, and outputs a se- 
quence of gesture predictions.

the police gesture dataset are played at normal speed, and 8 sub- 
jects with Chinese driving licences are asked to press a button as 
soon as they recognized a gesture. For each gesture, the response

response the police after he saw a whole gesture, which is a dura- 
tion of at least 2 s. Therefore, the response time of our method is 
within an acceptable range. 
We shorten the CPM network to decrease the execution time 
of each frame for real-time detection and recognition, which is an 
optimization that does not relevant to the response time discussed 
above. Theoretically, if the execution time of each frame became 
longer, to keep the real-time performance, we can just decrease 
the amount of frames per second to compensate. However, this 
also decrease the spatial features received per second, which re- 
sults in less reliable classiﬁcation outputs. Under the graphic card 
environment of Nvidia GTX 1080 TI, our method runs at 17.2 fps 
per second (we limited the speed at 15 fps for stability), receiving 
15 frames of spatial features within a second. As a comparison, if 
original CPM is applied, the whole program runs at 8.7 fps, which 
receives signiﬁcantly less spatial features (8–9 frames per second) 
when handling online tasks. Using ResNet as articulated pose ex- 
tractor can further decrease this to 5.1 fps. The lack of frames can 
ultimately cause more classiﬁcation errors to the online tasks. In 
summary, our method manage to achieve real-time performance 
with relatively more fps so as to keep a high accuracy when 
working online. 
7. Conclusion and future work 
In this paper, we integrate Convolutional Pose Machine with 
two kinds of handcrafted features: Relative Bone Length and An- 
gle with Gravity, and adopt a LSTM network to capture the tem- 
poral domain feature and recognize police gestures accurately. Our 
method also provides an architecture that is able to perform online 
police gesture recognition tasks with high accuracy. The method 
extracts skeletal data from image by a modiﬁed CPM to decrease 
the inﬂuence of background scenes. Then, it converts the skeletal 
data to two kinds of handcrafted spatial features: Relative Bone 
Length and Angle with Gravity to avoid the noises from absolute 
positions. At last, it sends the sequence of spatial features to LSTM 
network for the temporal features, and ﬁnally uses a dense con- 
nected network to infer the class of gestures. 
Future work will concentrate on recognizing traﬃc police ges- 
tures in multi-person scenes, and separating the traﬃc police from 
the crowd. This task includes recognizing multiple traﬃc police 
attire, and picking all the parts that belong to the police from a 
crowded scene. The dataset will be expanded as well to build the 
recognition model.
